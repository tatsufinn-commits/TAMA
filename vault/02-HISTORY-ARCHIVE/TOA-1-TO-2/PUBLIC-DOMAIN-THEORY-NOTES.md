## 1. Vitruvius — The Ten Books on Architecture (c. 15 BC; 1914 Morgan trans.)

- **The Vitruvian Triad (firmitas, utilitas, venustas):** architecture must possess **firmness** (structural soundness), **commodity/utility** (function), and **delight/beauty** (aesthetics). The most-cited definitional triad in architecture theory.
- First surviving systematic treatise on architecture; Book I covers the education of the architect; Books III–IV the classical orders; Book V public buildings; Book VI private houses; Book X machines.
- **"The architect should be educated in both theory and practice"** — the liberal-arts ideal (geometry, optics, music, etc.).
- Exam angle: definitions, the triad, orders (Doric/Ionic/Corinthian proportions), siting/orientation principles.

## 2. Alberti — De re aedificatoria (Ten Books, 1452; first printed 1485)

- First Renaissance treatise; reframes Vitruvius through humanist scholarship.
- Key concept: **concinnitas** — the harmonious integration of all parts; beauty as rational harmony.
- Distinguishes **lineamenta** (design/concept) from **structura** (material construction) — a foundational theory distinction.
- Exam angle: Renaissance theory; beauty as proportion/harmony; architect as intellectual.

## 3. Palladio — The Four Books of Architecture (1570)

- Systematic presentation of ancient Roman architecture + Palladio's own villa/palazzo designs with measured plans and elevations.
- **Palladian window**, villa typology (e.g., Villa Rotonda), the relationship of plan symmetry to classical orders.
- Exam angle: Renaissance design method; classical language; villa plans (studio precedent study).

## 4. Ruskin — The Seven Lamps of Architecture (1849)

- The "lamps" (principles): **Sacrifice, Truth, Power, Beauty, Life, Memory, Obedience**.
- Central ideas: architecture as moral act; **Truth** — honest expression of materials and structure (no sham construction); **Memory** — architecture as the carrier of history; Gothic as the truthful style.
- Exam angle: 19th-c. theory; morality in architecture; Gothic Revival rationale.

## 5. Owen Jones — The Grammar of Ornament (1856)

- Survey of ornament across cultures (Egyptian, Greek, Arabian, Indian, etc.) with color plates.
- **37 Propositions on ornament/color/form** (e.g., "the principles of arrangement of form and colour…"); Proposition 8: "All ornament should be based upon a geometrical construction."
- Exam angle: ornament theory; color; pattern; historicism in the 19th century.

## 6. Ware — The American Vignola (1904)

- Standard textbook of the **five classical orders** (Tuscan, Doric, Ionic, Corinthian, Composite) with measured proportions: column heights in diameters (e.g., Doric ≈ 7 diameters, Ionic ≈ 8, Corinthian ≈ 9 in the classical convention), entablature anatomy (architrave–frieze–cornice), pediments, arches, vaults.
- Exam angle: order identification; proportional system; studio drawing conventions.

## 7. Le Corbusier — Towards a New Architecture (1923/1927)

- Manifesto of Modernism: **"a house is a machine for living in"**; the engineer's aesthetic; five points of architecture (pilotis, roof garden, free plan, ribbon window, free façade); mass, surface, plan.
- Exam angle: Modern Movement theory; functionalism; contrast with classical tradition.

## 8. Banister Fletcher — A History of Architecture on the Comparative Method

- The classic one-volume chronological survey: **"The Tree of Architecture"** (style branches) and the comparative method (geography, geology, climate, religion, social/political, historical influences per period).
- Exam angle: chronological framework for HOA; the "influences" formula (Period → Context → Characteristics → Architects → Works → Influence).
